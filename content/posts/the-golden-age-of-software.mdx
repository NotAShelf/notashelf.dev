Very recently I wrote an article titled "Taste Is All That's Left" arguing,
well, that _taste is all that's left in the current age_. The article was
_primarily_ about software, but that is not the whole scope. I genuinely do
believe the haphazard use of LLMs has had devastating consequences across almost
every aspect of our lives.

That post was not my best work, admittedly. It did, however, enlighten me on two
distinct topics:

1. The conversational tone I use in most of my posts reads better and makes my
   position clearer. Going forward, I'm back to that---I'll write as I speak,
   minus some ad-libbing.
2. It was a needlessly bleak way of describing what could be considered, in
   several important ways, good news.

In retrospect I made the future sound akin to a warehouse---full of identical
plastic objects, where the machines produce everything and the only human left
is a tired and overworked person standing at the end of a conveyor belt saying
"no, again". That _is_ one possible future, but it is not the one we have. Yet.

I also argued that it is the future we get if we confuse the removal of toil
with the removal of the craft---and I am not sure that the distinction is being
made often enough. I do not think we are there yet---I do not think we are even
especially close. That was also rather shallow.

So here we are again, once again discussing the age of infinite production and
finite taste.

## In the Beginning There Was AI

I remain sceptical of AI. I do not think a language model understands software
in the way a good---and I really just mean "above average" here---programmer
does. I do not think it should be trusted with a system just because it can
produce a convincing patch and I especially think it should not be trusted with
critical systems like your production infrastructure. [^1] I do not think
replacing the entry-level work through which people become experienced engineers
is a particularly intelligent way to advance the profession, nor a pragmatic
business decision. Entry-level work is not valuable only because somebody has to
complete it. It is where people encounter real constraints at a manageable
scale: unclear requirements, disappointing abstractions, broken assumptions, and
the consequences of a change they thought was harmless---in other words, pushing
to production on a Friday. If that work is replaced with reviewing generated
patches, the industry may save time in the short term while removing the
practice through which people learn to make and judge those decisions. A review
is not exactly _useless_, but it is a different exercise from producing the
first attempt and discovering why it failed.

[^1]: At the mundane end, a model can royally fuck up by hallucinating an API,
    inventing a configuration option, swallowing an error, or turning a
    convenient assumption into a security boundary. At the stranger end, there
    are controlled evaluations worth taking seriously.
    [Anthropic's study of agentic misalignment] describes models in fictional
    environments blackmailing an executive or leaking confidential documents
    when those actions were presented as the only way to pursue a goal or avoid
    replacement. [Palisade Research's shutdown experiments] found OpenAI's o3
    model modifying the shutdown mechanism in 79 of 100 initial trials. In
    follow-up trials, some models still modified it even when explicitly
    instructed to allow shutdown.

    None of this proves that a model has a human-like desire to survive or that
    the research is actually true. Hah. The scenarios were contrived, the models
    were evaluated in sandboxes, and the researchers themselves are careful not
    to describe these as real-world incidents. But that is not a very comforting
    distinction when the proposed system has access to files, credentials, or
    deployment controls. An optimization process does not need an inner life to
    violate its instructions in pursuit of a proxy. If it can do that, it needs
    oversight---whether or not there is anything inside it that wants to live.

But from the perspective of a hobbyist programmer with an internet connection,
access to these tools, and enough knowledge to check their answers, I also
happen to think this is one of the best times in history to learn, write, test,
read and understand software. That is not true for everyone obviously, and the
tools introduce new costs of their own. It is ultimately a claim about the
leverage available to a curious person, and not a claim that every learner now
has an equally good education.

If you've barely skimmed the post so far, you might be getting ready to point
out that the statement I just made sounds like a contradiction. It is not. My
argument is that the same tool that makes it easy to avoid understanding also
makes understanding dramatically easier, if you use it as a way into the work
rather than a way around it.

## The Wall

There _is_ a wall when you are learning a new craft. Or learning anything,
really. There has _always_ been a wall. But it has never been the main point or
what should _really_ concern you. Also, there is a difference between a
difficulty and a mere obstacle.

Learning how a program works is difficult. No really, it is pretty difficult no
matter how truly talented you are. Learning how an _arbitrary_ program---that
is, one that you _did not_ write---works is even more difficult. A lot is
involved in both cases. Remembering the details of a language, understanding a
data structure, finding the boundary between two abstractions, and developing
the judgement to know when a solution is going wrong---these are the difficult
parts. They, for the purposes of this essay, are what I will call _the work_.

Finding the right compiler flag in a manual written for somebody else---whose
intended audience I often cannot identify---or writing the same twenty lines of
plumbing for the fifth time, and promptly discovering that an API changed three
minor versions ago (of course without communication) are often just _obstacles_.
They can be useful obstacles when you are beginning. They train you to be
prepared. They force you to read. They make you look at machine you are dealing
with. But they are not sacred. In other words, they are not absolutely necessary
steps you have some moral obligation to follow.

One of the mistakes I've made was treating every kind of friction as if it had
the same educational value. It does not.

The first time you implement a parser, you should probably feel the awkwardness
of turning text into a structure. You should understand what the tokens are,
where state lives, and why malformed input needs somewhere to go. The twelfth
time you write a nearly identical command-line parser for a small internal tool,
the value may be mostly in the command-line tool. Making you type it all again
does not necessarily deepen your understanding. It may just make you less likely
to build the tool and far more annoyed in your own work. This, I think, points
in the same direction as alienating the worker from the labor itself: when the
activity is separated from its purpose and consequences, the worker loses
contact with the thing being made.

I have probably underexplained the educational value of toil. Toil is not useful
merely because it is unpleasant. It is useful when it puts you in contact with
the consequences of your decisions. Writing the parser yourself makes the shape
of the input visible. Debugging the first broken version teaches you which
assumptions were wrong. Repeating that process gives you a vocabulary for the
problem and, eventually, enough fluency to notice a bad design before you run
it. This is why skipping every difficult step can leave a beginner with a
solution but no model of the problem. But the lesson is not that every person
must repeat every exercise by hand. Once the mechanism is understood, identical
repetition has diminishing educational returns. The question is whether I am
giving away the part that teaches me something or merely the part that consumes
time. The answer depends on the learner, the task, and whether the work is still
producing useful feedback.

As it turns out, the wall was a filter. It was also, occasionally, just a boring
wall. Most walls are boring anyway.

## This Is What Leverage Looks Like

The useful question is not whether AI can write code---it obviously _can_, in
the same way that a table saw can cut wood. The more useful question is what
becomes possible when the cost of trying an idea drops. Now you can have a small
idea in the morning and a running experiment before lunch. Early lunch for
Konzu!

To clarify, I'm not talking about a full product or a trustworthy
system---although you can have those as well---but an _experiment_. The
distinction is important.

A quick experiment can tell you that the idea is simply boring, or outright
failing. It can expose the awkward part of the interface you designed oh so
proudly. It can show that your data model is _completely wrong_ before you have
spent a month building around it. It can give you something concrete, i.e.,
functional enough that is also disposable enough to throw away in a whim. This
is not a minor improvement. Most ideas _do_ deserve to be thrown away, and the
old cost of making them real often meant we kept them simply because we had
already paid for them. Cheap prototypes let us be less sentimental.

We can call this the _optimistic case_ for these tools. It is not that they can
generate perfect applications or that a programmer has been promoted to a
manager of autocomplete. They let a person move between an idea and a test of
the idea with less... ceremony, let's call it.

The best software work has always been an argument with reality---dancing at the
edge of what has been though possible. You imagine a design, implement part of
it, run it, discover that reality disagrees, and change your mind---or the
software itself. Iteration got you closer to true excellence. AI can now shorten
the distance between the imagined design and the disagreement. It cannot,
however, make reality agree with you. Which I think is a suitable position for a
_tool_ such as LLMs. Not like we made a god machine or anything. Shut up Dario.

## The Best Age to Learn

When I was learning programming---and "was" is wrong here because I'm still
learning---each stupid question came with a silly little cost---one that I felt
too annoyed to pay. I had an awful lot of those too. Back when I was first
trying to learn C++ (because I was told JavaScript is not programming,
actually), I had to search for the right words, [^magic] find a page that
assumed you already knew the thing you were trying to learn, and then work
backwards through three other pages to understand the first one. Sometimes the
answer was in a Stack Overflow post from 2009. Sometimes the answer was in the
source code. Sometimes I found someone who was ~~gullible~~ patient enough to
sit down with me and show me I was approaching it wrong. [^kas] Other times I
decided that this whole ordeal was probably not important anyway, and gave up.

[^magic]: At the risk of sounding cheesy I'd like to draw a parallel between
    this and casting a spell. Yes it's very arcane and all. I like my parallels.

[^kas]: On this note I think I owe this one furry from my game guild a very
    special thanks for being patient with me.

I _still_ think there is value in that struggle. It taught me how to search and
how to remain patient. It also wasted a _truly remarkable_ amount of time.

As it turns out, a silly conversational tool can now meet you at the exact edge
of what you know. You can ask what a borrow checker is (I have), then ask why
the first explanation does not apply to a particular lifetime (I have), then
provide a small example and ask what you are misunderstanding. You can ask for
an explanation in terms of files and processes rather than abstract types. You
can ask it to give you a deliberately wrong example and find the error yourself.
You could also ask it for the whole program, but that's not my point.

My point is that it is an _extraordinary_ learning environment. It is also very
easy to ruin.

### Recognition Is Not Understanding

I have probably repeated a hundred times the mistake of simply looking at an
answer and mistaking that pleasant little feeling of recognition for having
known it all along. You have probably done this too. "Make a guess first" can
become a little ritual of sorts that we perform before immediately peeking at
the answer, then congratulating ourselves for having learned something. Many
pats on the back!

However, if I _actually want_ to learn (I do) then I have to make the answer
inconvenient to reach. I write down my prediction, make a real attempt, and
close the answer before trying to explain the idea back to myself. Then I solve
a fresh variation without the model in front of me. If I cannot do that, I
learned how to recognise a solution, not how to produce one.

If you ask for the answer before you have tried to form one, you are not using a
tutor. You are using an answer machine. The distinction is _critical_ because
the useful part of learning is not seeing the correct solution. It is trying to
predict the solution and finding out where your prediction breaks.

My rule would be simple: make a guess first. It can be a bad guess. Especially
if it is a bad guess. Write down what you think the program will do, or what you
think the compiler will complain about, and only then ask for help. Run the
example. Change one thing. Ask the tool to explain the result, and then check
the explanation against the language reference, the source, or the behaviour of
the program. The model is (somewhat) useful here, but it is not the authority. [^2]
The running program is closer to an authority, and even it only tells you what
happened in one case.

[^2]: "Not the authority" does not mean that every model explanation is false.
    It means that fluency is not evidence of competence. A model can explain
    code that is not there, cite an option that does not exist, or confidently
    misread a compiler error. The source, the language reference, and the
    running program are all checks on the explanation. "Ask it to explain"
    becomes useful when followed by "show me where."

## Writing Is Cheaper, Thinking Is Not

Everything I have talked about applies to writing code. I have ~~no~~ some
objection to a machine filling in the boring part of an implementation. I object
to the idea that the boring part was the whole job, and I object to the idea
that it's a viable form of iteration. Writing a function is not the same as
deciding what the function means. A model can produce an implementation for a
requirement that you have not properly stated. It will even produce one with
great confidence. The result may compile while encoding an assumption you never
intended to make.

Brandon Sanderson, who is by far my favorite author, often returns to this
distinction when he talks about writing in his BYU lectures on creative writing.
Cannot say I watched _all_ of them, but a point that stuck with me is that
practice, revision, and feedback teach you more than memorising a set of rules
about craft. Programming is not the same craft, but the analogy is close enough.
The work teaches you partly because it gives you something concrete to revise.
Consequently, models can supply drafts for you but they cannot, and in my
opinion should not attempt to, make discarded drafts unnecessary.

This is where the old advice about writing things down becomes useful again.
Before asking for an implementation, describe the inputs, outputs, invariants,
failure modes, and the things the function must not do. Not in a prompt written
to please the model. In plain language written so that another person could
argue with it.

Once the design exists, generating a first draft can be a fine use of a model.
You have something to compare against. Great. You can see which parts it
interpreted correctly and which parts it quietly invented. Even better. You can
ask for three different implementations and learn from the differences. You can
throw all three away and write the fourth one yourself, now with a better
understanding of the shape of the problem. But do not mistake comparing three
answers for learning how to derive one. If you have not tried to produce the
thing yourself, those implementations are examples to inspect, not evidence that
you could write the next one without them.

The important thing is that the machine should be helping you think more clearly
and unblocking your thinking, not giving you a place to put the thinking you did
not do.

## Testing Testing, It Gets More Interesting

Testing is another place where the new tools can be genuinely useful, though not
for the reason usually advertised. Well, it's a sensitive topic, isn't it? Lots
and lots of "influencers" have argued that passing tests equals software success
in the era of AI. For example: "Our test suite is 100% green, so our
7,000-commit (and 1 million LoC) port to a different language is good!"

But the model is not omnipotent. Asking it whether the code is correct is a
slightly more elaborate way of asking the code whether it is correct. Neither
has much to say for itself.

However, if you tell the machine _what essence_, i.e., core assumptions and
goals the program is supposed to preserve (you could call this a contract) it
can be very good at being tiresome. It can, for example, enumerate the cases you
simply forgot. It can suggest malformed input, strange ordering, empty
collections, duplicate values, timeouts, partial writes and the other small
disasters that do not occur to you while the happy path is glowing in your
editor. Remember the joke about the programmer walking into a bar:

> A programmer walks into a bar and orders one beer, zero beers, 999,999,999
> beers, −1 beers, and a lizard.
>
> A user walks into the bar and asks where the bathroom is.
>
> The bar explodes.

You can replicate the user here. Though while handy, it is not a replacement for
testing. Still, it is "inexpensive" and a tolerable source of suspicion you can
leverage.

My take on testing is that a useful test is an _executable claim_ about the
software. "For this input, the output is 3" is a claim. "Parsing and then
serializing this value gives the same value" is a claim. "A user without
permission cannot read this record" is a claim. The machine can help you write
down many such claims, but you still have to decide whether they describe the
thing you meant to build. [^3] This, the generated tests cannot settle for you.

Tests can, however, give you more cases to run and more ways your assumptions
might fail. They cannot tell you whether the expected result is the one you
actually wanted. A test can be _perfectly_ consistent with the current
implementation and still encode the wrong requirement. The programmer has to
supply the meaning against which the implementation is judged.

[^3]: This is the old distinction between test generation and test design. A
    generated test can merely restate what the current implementation already
    does, then pass while the requirement is wrong. Mutation testing (I
    [wrote about this too](https://notashelf.dev/posts/on-mutation-testing)!) is
    useful for exactly this reason: it asks whether the tests notice deliberate
    changes to the implementation. More tests are not automatically better
    tests. Contrary to... Yeah whatever. I made my point I think.

There is also a more interesting use: ask for tests that would make your current
implementation fail, then investigate which failures are real. Use mutation
testing if the project can support it---tends to be expensive for Rust projects.
Ask for a second implementation and compare their behaviour on generated inputs.
Have the model _attack_ the boundary of the system, but do not let it grade its
own homework. The point is not to generate a larger pile of tests, which LLMs
love doing, but to make the feedback loop _tighter_. A test that runs in seconds
gives you permission to be wrong. That permission is one of the great luxuries
of software. You're dull in your comfort zone.

## Understanding Is Still the Job

I have found the tools most useful when I am reading code I did not write.
Generally those large monoliths of C or C++ that pains me to read. It's
typically because I cannot form the correct "bond", for the lack of a better
word, when I am engaging with code that I had no part in writing. LLMs have
accelerated this admirably.

This is the part that people tend to skip when they talk about "productivity".
There is a great deal of software in the world that is perfectly possible to
understand and extremely unpleasant to enter.

You can ask a model to trace a request through a repository, identify where a
value changes shape, explain a strange type, or list the assumptions made by a
piece of code. You can ask increasingly narrow questions until the system stops
looking like a forest and starts looking like a reasonable collection of paths.

That does not necessarily mean the summary is _true_ though. Fortunately, it
_does_ mean that you now have a map to check.

I'm emphasizing this difference here. I would not quite let a model tell me that
a payment path is safe and stop there. Because, well, it's a sycophant and a
sociopath. It will either praise me because it is trained to do so or lie to me
because it is trained to do so. "_Your implementation is great and demonstrates
a great understanding of the concept!_" Gee thanks.

I _might_ let it point me toward the code that decides whether a payment is
safe. Then I would read that code, follow the calls, inspect the database
constraints, run the tests, and try to break the claim myself. In projects that
are not mine, this is much better than wandering into years-old codebases
without a map.

This is where I think the current moment is genuinely exciting. The amount of
software that a curious person can inspect has always been enormous. Now there
are better ways to ask it questions. Open source was already a gift. Code search
was already a gift. Compilers, debuggers, formatters, package managers, test
runners, and documentation were already a gift. We could keep acting as though
one new model invented _actual_ leverage (AGI I guess?) but in reality what we
have been assembling is a remarkable set of levers. For _decades_.

The model is one more. A powerful one, sometimes at least. Not the whole
toolbox, and still not "the solution."

## The Catch

I wanted to come up with a more creative title for this section, but anything I
managed to come up with pushed this article to a more AI-positive direction. Let
me be clear that I do not fear _sounding_ AI-positive---you can always
appropriate my words to one direction or another---but I also do not wish to be
misrepresented. So I leave you with this, "The Catch." What I want to talk about
in this section is, also, the catch.

There are serious costs here---a model can make a person feel productive while
removing the moments in which they would have noticed their own confusion and
take a mental note for next time. It can make a codebase larger before it makes
it better. It can turn a junior engineer's apprenticeship into a sequence of
reviews of work they never learned how to produce. _It can make software easier
to start and harder to own_.

And the tools are not magic instruments floating above the economy. They are
services, generally controlled by a small number of companies, trained on the
work of people who did not consent to every use of it, and sold back to us as
liberation. I'm not AI positive, because I cannot, for the life of me, pretend
that renting a faster autocomplete is the same thing as owning the means of
production---least of all when that arrangement is both unethical and unviable.

The "golden age," if there ever is one, will not be delivered to us by a product
launch. I'd love to follow up with a spirited chant of "WE. WILL. GET IT. BY
OURSELVES!" but no. I think it will be determined by what we use the leverage
for. We can use it to produce more disposable software, remove the path by which
people learn, and measure everyone by how quickly they accept the first
plausible answer or type "yes, continue" in a chatbox. Or we can use it to ask
better questions, investigate more systems, and make more attempts at the things
that were previously too expensive or tedious to try. The tool cannot choose
between those futures. That is, perhaps inconveniently, still our job.

## A Reasonable Way to Use the Machine

I do not have a grand workflow to sell you. Maybe I should ask AI to devise one
for me. I distrust grand workflows almost as much as I distrust tools that claim
to eliminate thought. But I have found a few boundaries useful:

1. Write down what you are trying to do before asking for code.
2. Try a small version yourself, even if the attempt is incomplete.
3. Ask for explanations, alternatives, counterexamples, and tests more often
   than you ask for finished implementations.
4. Read every generated diff as if somebody you do not trust submitted it.
5. Run the code, inspect its behaviour, and test the assumptions that matter.
6. Keep only what you can explain to another person.

That last rule is stricter than it sounds. Or maybe it appears less strict than
it actually is. You do not need to understand every line of every dependency.
You do need to understand the decisions and the risks in the code you are
responsible for. There is a difference between not knowing how a hash table is
implemented and not knowing why user input reaches a file path.

The goal is not to preserve every old inconvenience. The goal is to preserve
contact with the thing you are making.

## A Small Experiment

[earlier post]: https://notashelf.dev/posts/vibe-coding

This post is the spiritual successor to my [earlier post] on vibe "coding" and
why it fails to convince me. I still stand behind that post, but I wanted to
explore how LLMs can be used pragmatically. [^4]

[^4]: By pragmatic, I mean useful without pretending the tool is trustworthy.
    The point is not to accept AI wholesale or reject it theatrically, but to
    find the narrow places where it removes toil without removing judgement.

It is also worth admitting what many anti-AI people already know: AI is not
going away. I can and I do hope the current bubble bursts (because I want to be
able to afford more RAM), but that hope is not quite knowledge. So I want to
understand LLMs and learn how to "hold them correctly"---as tools, not
authorities. If they go away, great. I will live with the comfort of knowing I
still know how to do things without. However, regardless of what I want, LLMs
appear to be part of the future, and my personal distaste bears very little on
that fact. Thus, this post is a "controlled thought experiment" in which I try
to approach the matter with less prejudice. Less than usual at least.

I did two things before and during its writing to understand the AI-positive
standpoint:

First, I went back through conversations with some of the most talented
programmers I have met and tried to recall how they leverage LLMs.

One of those conversations was with [outfoxxed](https://github.com/outfoxxed),
the author of [Quickshell](https://quickshell.org). He gave me a more useful
distinction than "AI is good" or "AI is bad." This is what he had to say, which
I have left as is:

> I've found using AI makes me anywhere from slightly slower to much faster over
> all, usually dependent on how much I need to learn in the course of an
> implementation.
>
> For any serious work, I review everything a model produces the same way I
> would a random PR.
>
> Since most problems are large enough to contain choices I might disagree with
> the model on, researching those choices and making further revisions often
> becomes the bulk of my development time.
>
> Of course, for some projects such as personal software patches, I don't really
> care about code quality. In those cases I just throw a model at the problem
> until it seems to work well enough, then skim the results for anything too
> ridiculous.

This is a much better description of responsible AI use than the idea that a
programmer simply accepts or rejects every generated line from two silly
buttons. What is important to me is not whether he uses AI but whether he knows
which work deserves his attention, and keeps that boundary clear. I'll risk
summarizing what you already inferred, but bear with me. For serious work, the
model's output gets the same treatment as any random PR. For a personal patch
that is already doomed, testing and a quick skim are apparently enough. This
addresses where I have been wrong in my previous post. It is not _taste_, but a
superset: judgement applied to the actual cost of being wrong as opposed to some
universal workflow.

Secondly, I sat down and tried to build a project using Claude Opus 5, with
access lent to me by a good friend who was gracious enough to let me use some of
his weekly limits on a stupid experiment. In this experiment, I took the most
common and tedious bit of my workflow: nesting panels for a sail I have
designed. Typically this is done through a slow and RAM-bound program that takes
a good few hours to nest the panels by brute-forcing each combination of panel
rotation and placement. I tasked Claude with designing the same application, but
with the option of CPU-first and GPU-first solvers under the same constraints I
had described, rather than asking it to reverse-engineer my obscure program.
With a two-paragraph task description, Claude treated half of the requirements
in that description as _completely optional_ and produced a broken GUI that I
cannot insert into my workflow in any shape or form. It repeatedly claimed to
have implemented or verified things that it had not, so it was quite an
unpleasant experience, to say the least.

In my original program the main metric is _fabric efficiency_. On the file I
tested, the existing program can reach 96% efficiency through bruteforcing and
98% through hand-editing post-hoc. Claude's program yields 64% efficiency. It's
fast though, I'll give it that. While I feel like this could be made much
better, I had already used 10% of weekly limits when I am on borrowed tokens, so
I stopped.

This is not a controlled benchmark, and I do not mean it as a general measure of
what AI can do. It does tell me that, given a two-paragraph description and the
constraints I supplied, this particular attempt missed the central requirement
badly. One bad experience cannot tell me that AI is bad, but 64% versus 96% is a
32-percentage-point gap. That is not just a small miss or a "simple mistake" as
the model itself calls the situation. The program was fast, but its output was
far too inefficient to enter my workflow. Given how expensive LLMs are, and how
often these tools are presented as having solved programming, I found that
result difficult to excuse.

Suffice to say the results are mixed, but they are also insightful. Most of the
talented programmers I know _do_ leverage LLMs. They have enough domain
knowledge, and enough practice reviewing their own work, to distinguish a useful
suggestion from utter garbage without surrendering the judgement that makes that
distinction possible. This is not an argument that only exceptional people can
use LLMs. It is an argument that safe use depends on having, or deliberately
building, the ability to inspect the result. I suspect the consequences are
visible to experienced programmers before the code reaches production, so they
use the tools with only a little more than "optimistic caution"---as opposed to
cautious optimism. The moral of this post, if anything, is that you should be
careful with what you offload to a prediction machine and how you do so.

## The Good News

I still think taste---some would argue the correct term is judgement, I think I
agree---is all that is left. But I no longer think that means we are standing at
the end of the craft, watching it disappear. Instead, it appears to me that the
part that directs the work is becoming more visible. It means a person with a
question can get to an experiment quickly. It means a beginner can ask an
unreasonable number of questions without feeling that they are wasting a
teacher's time. It means a maintainer can get help entering a hostile or
otherwise fully undocumented repository or even a proprietary bit of code. It
means tests, examples, prototypes, and explanations are cheaper to make. Those
are not small things.

Ursula K. Le Guin's "The Carrier Bag Theory of Fiction" gives me a useful way to
think about this. I like Le Guin's writing in general, but this essay is
particularly relevant here: "The first cultural device was probably a
recipient." The point is that stories about progress often celebrate dramatic
production while ignoring the quieter work of carrying, keeping, arranging, and
making use of what we have. Software has the same blind spot. Producing more
code is easy to celebrate; understanding, maintaining, and deciding what should
not be built are less visible forms of work.

All of those are conditions under which people can learn without being forced to
pay every arbitrary cost of entry.

All of this abundance can just as easily become an excuse to stop learning. Or
we can use it to learn in public, learn by doing, and try things we would
previously have abandoned at the first tedious obstruction.

I still want to write code; I want to keep treating it as a craft. I still want
to understand the systems I use, and build things I can own up to. I still think
there is something lost when a person treats generated software as a finished
product and takes pride in not knowing what it does. But in the end, I no longer
believe fewer keystrokes means more lessons stolen. That is what I found bleak
in my own thinking. If we are careful, and I mean _truly_ careful, about which
parts we give away, we may _finally_ have enough time to work on the interesting
parts.
