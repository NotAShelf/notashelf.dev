Yesterday---technically less than 24 hours ago---I published something about
taste, and it did what writing on the internet does. A good part of the response
read it as a _scolding_---as though I had stood over people newer to this than
me and criticized their lack of taste. I will clarify this once, _and only
once_: that was not the argument, and I do not think people lack taste. [^1] I
have no interest in ranking anyone's. At least not yours, random internet
stranger.

[^1]: The post was
    ["Taste Is All That's Left"](https://notashelf.dev/posts/taste-is-all-thats-left).
    If you want the longer version of that clarification, I have amended a
    post-mortem section after this exact misreading played out in the comments.

What the response handed to me was a subject _much better_ than the one I
started with. Most of the surrounding argument was not about taste at all, but
about whether it was a _real person_ that had written the words on the screen.
That is a question about _language_, and it is the one I want to spend this post
on.

## You and I Did Not Invent It

Here is a completely irrelevant thought experiment: if you (but _only_ you) were
to be dropped on an island today with the entirety of your knowledge fully
intact, how much of the common technology you are accustomed to could you
conjure from scratch---in a cave with a bunch of scraps? Could you Robinson
Crusoe your way into an existence that is not merely surviving, but thriving in
its own right?

While you're thinking about the answer, I'll go ahead and start with the main
thesis of this paper and perhaps something that sits too close to notice: you
did not invent your language. _Not one word of it_. Well yes, you might have
"invented" some bits as a child that your parents pretend to have understood,
but every phrase you are proud of, every construction you think of as yours,
every pattern that found its way into how you speak---you assembled all of it
out of pieces you heard somewhere once and kept. You carry a version of it that
is about all you can carry. In other words, the most private version of
this---your idiolect, the dialect spoken by exactly one person---is still built
entirely from borrowed material. [^2]

[^2]: _Idiolect_ is the linguist's term for the language of a single
    individual---your own private inventory of words, pronunciations, and
    constructions. No two people share one, and nobody builds theirs from
    scratch. If you are wondering why I know this term, you can thank Hideo
    Kojima (brilliant as always!) for putting the term _lingua franca_ into
    Metal Gear Solid V, which sent me down a rabbit hole that eventually turned
    up this one.

Ferdinand de Saussure split it, rather cleanly, a century ago as _langue_, the
shared system that nobody owns, and _parole_, the individual act of speaking it.
You only ever hold the second. The first lives in the common space between all
of us, and it drifts constantly because everyone who has ever handled it leaves
a mark. It is a thing so much larger than any single speaker that looking
straight at it makes me a little megalophobic.

## Let's Build a Language

What fascinates me is how a language forms when there is not one to reach for
yet. No committee designs it. People build it out of fragments, because they
need to understand each other _efficiently_ and have nothing standard to use.
Naturally, something has to exist.

There are two particular cases I return to. The first is old: after Alexander's
conquests threw speakers of different Greek dialects together, the differences
sanded down into a shared, neutral variety. It is called _Koine_, which simply
means "common." No one drafted it. It averaged itself into being out of the
daily need to trade and govern across dialects. [^3]

[^3]: The mechanism has a name in modern sociolinguistics: _koineization_, the
    leveling and mixing that happens when speakers of different but mutually
    intelligible varieties are thrown together. Peter Trudgill's
    [_New-Dialect Formation_](https://books.google.com/books/about/New_dialect_Formation.html?id=MuMqnV_e9WAC)
    is the standard treatment.

The second is recent enough that linguists got to watch it happen. In Nicaragua,
across the late 1970s and 1980s, deaf children were brought together in schools
for the first time with no common language among them. Within a generation they
had produced one---a full sign language, with grammar those children were never
taught, handed down and sharpened by each younger cohort until it was
unmistakably a language and not a bag of gestures. [^4] A language born in a
schoolyard, from nothing, because a community needed to be understood.

[^4]: Idioma de Señas de Nicaragua. Its emergence was documented by Judy Kegl,
    Ann Senghas, and Marie Coppola, among others; Senghas and Coppola's
    ["Children Creating Language"](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00359)
    (_Psychological Science_, 2001) is a good entry point. Each incoming cohort
    of younger children regularized the grammar further than the one
    before---the language grew more systematic as it was passed down, not less.

Both point at the same thing. Language is emergent and collective. It is a
group's need to understand one another, settling into a shape. Neutral in the
honest sense of the word: it belongs to the group and to no single person in it.
Though this also makes me think. Could I do the same? Could _we_ do the same? I
am not entirely certain if the answer is "yes, absolutely."

## The Composite

Which brings me, unavoidably, to the machine. A large language model is trained
on a vast pile of that collective output and learns to produce its center of
mass. [^5] It has no idiolect; it has the opposite---the koine of everyone at
once, a million idiolects averaged together with the idiosyncrasy filed off. So
when a reader looks at a paragraph of it and says _no real person writes like
this_, they are correct, and for an exact reason. It is not a person, but the
composite of all of them.

[^5]: The phrase I keep for this is "stochastic parrot," from Bender, Gebru,
    McMillan-Major, and Mitchell's
    [2021 paper](https://doi.org/10.1145/3442188.3445922)---a system that
    stitches together plausible sequences from its training distribution with no
    grasp of meaning. You can argue with the paper's conclusions and still find
    the image exact.

Faces do the same trick. Blend many photographs of faces into a single composite
and the result comes out smoother, more symmetrical, and reliably rated more
attractive than nearly any of the faces that went into it---and faintly uncanny,
because it is no one at all. [^6] Averaged prose is that composite face rendered
in words: fluent, clean, familiar, and unsettlingly person-less.

[^6]: Langlois and Roggman,
    ["Attractive Faces Are Only Average"](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1990.tb00079.x)
    (_Psychological Science_, 1990). Digitally averaged composite faces were
    judged more attractive than the individual faces blended into them. The
    averaging strips away asymmetry and idiosyncrasy---the same things that make
    a face, or a sentence, feel like it came from someone in particular.

## Both Directions

This is why the confusion runs both ways, and why it is so easy in each. It is
trivial to mistake the machine's writing for a person's, because it was built
out of persons. And it is now just as easy to mistake a person's writing for the
machine's---because the patterns we are learning to call tells were ours to
begin with. The em-dash. The three-part list. The balanced sentence that turns
on itself. The short line, dropped for weight. The model invented none of it. It
took all of it from us, at a scale no person could match, and handed it back so
evenly that we started to read our own habits as its fingerprints.

See what I did there? The paragraph before one is that reads wrong. Should I fix
it? Probably. _Can_ I fix it? Well, that much is unclear to me. When I cut those
things out of my own writing now---and this time, I did---I am half disowning my
own language, because a machine learned to speak it well enough to be mistaken
for me. No longer.

I have learned to be careful with my language, but this kind of caution is not
healthy. Now I catch myself mid-sentence, hovering over an em-dash, wondering
whether it is _truly_ mine or something I caught from a machine that caught it
from someone. By the time I decide, the thought has gone cold. Try writing
anything with a censor reading over your shoulder. Let me tell you: it does not
go well. And the paradox is rather cruel. Go ahead and strip out every habit
that reads like the machine, and what is left still reads like the machine,
because the machine assembled those habits from people who had already stopped
sounding like anyone in particular. So, no. I think I will keep the em-dash. I
was here---writing badly, maybe, but badly in a way that is at least mine.

## No Clean Ending

I do not have a tidy close for this, and I feel it is for the best that I do not
manufacture one. Call it a description and leave it there.

Language is borrowed, shared, and always drifting, and we have now built a
machine that speaks the average of the whole of it back to us. Standing in front
of that is a strange experience. Part of what it reflects is the machine. Part
of it, less comfortably, is us---seen from far enough back that the individual
falls away and only the common is left.

## Post Scriptum

I usually reserve the last section on a paragraph or two containing my closing
thoughts---things I want to leave you with and treat as a proper sendoff. Said
section is where I shake off the professional tone and be honest and direct with
the reader. Since this essay, as a whole, is _partially_ my "closing thoughts"
on the post that came before it, I am omitting that section here. It is still
honest and direct, but what follows is closer to summary or rebuttal of the
discussion I had during and after the storyboarding. I
have---naturally---received some comments, and while I could not fit them into
the post proper, I wish to address them nevertheless. So, outside the holistic
writeup, I will leave you with three claims I cannot quite prove and you cannot
quite refute. Though, that is what makes them fun.

1. This is not a defense, a rebuttal, or any kind of "response" to the negative
   comments I have received on the previous post. I do not feel obliged to
   defend myself. Like most people, I find myself equipped with _reasonable_
   taste, and I have already clarified that the goal of the post was not to
   lecture. You may believe this, you may not. I have taken your criticism, and
   I have said my piece.
2. I find language fascinating, and _machine learning_ even more so. If you are
   here after reading the previous post and could not _quite grasp_ what my
   stance is, then I will clarify: I am neither pro-AI nor against it
   altogether.
3. I am not a software engineer, and I am not claiming to be some omnipotent
   narrator who has identified the zeitgeist of the industry. This is a blog,
   and I share _my opinions_ on, you guessed it, _my blog_. The post before this
   reflects my views on the software scene as someone who partakes in it. This
   piece is a more personal reflection on how language operates, born of my
   curiosity and the snap back to reality that followed.

During the post-hoc discussions---and the writing of this post that followed---I
have found time to reflect on my _own_ writing. Clearly, I have not been doing a
good enough job of judging what is slop and what is not. Some users have pointed
out the irony of that, and prompted me to reflect. While I do not appreciate the
language, I do appreciate the sentiment.

With all of that out of the way, now it is time for me to get some rest. Be
well.
